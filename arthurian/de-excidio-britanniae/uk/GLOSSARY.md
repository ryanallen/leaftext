[^how-all-checks-passed]: Every range was run through the book's own check tool and the shelf's checks, and nothing was ticked done until they passed: a missing page, a broken link, an old word, a word repeated too soon, a wrong mark and a change nobody logged are all refused.

[^how-readable-first]: Each passage is told the way a good English writer would tell it today, then checked against the source so every event, person and act is there and nothing is invented.

[^how-plain-speech]: No Bible cadence and no stiff old-translation voice: no chains of "and… and… and", no solemn word order, no word nobody says now.

[^how-shaped-for-english]: Each sentence is written as English, not in the source's grammar: a verb rather than a noun made of one, never a clause hanging off a clause, and what someone must do before where or why, with nothing lost. Every sentence that says a common verb twice was read and either rewritten or kept with the reason.

[^how-every-link-said]: Where the source joins a cause and its effect with a bare "and", the English says how they connect, with "to", "by", "so", "until" or "because".

[^how-nothing-said-twice]: What the source says twice, a doubled verb or a speech the narration has just told, is said once, unless a speaker repeats it on purpose, and the book's account of its text is given once, its edition and scans cited in full only under Sources.

[^how-the-right-word-each-time]: A source word with several senses takes the English its moment needs, never one word everywhere and never the vaguest.

[^how-no-word-worn-out]: No word comes back within forty words of itself, names and the commonest grammar words aside; the second use takes another word or goes.

[^how-modern-idiom]: Old English turns give way to the ones people use now, in how people talk to and about each other as much as in rare words: "pregnant", "said goodbye", "my mom". Every word judged old is kept in the shelf's list, and the check refuses each one.

[^how-us-english]: The reviewed US master uses Merriam-Webster spelling and usage, the article a US reader says, as "an herb", *The Chicago Manual of Style*, and lengths and distances in feet and miles. A later UK edition is made from that master.

[^how-natural-tenses]: The story is told in the simple past wherever the order of events is plain, as English tells it, not in the source's stacked past tenses.

[^how-neutral-tone]: No archaism and no color the source doesn't give, but a hard word stays hard.

[^how-gender-neutral]: Where the source means anyone, the English says "they", "people" or "anyone", never "he" and never a pair of pronouns; a figure the source names keeps the pronoun the source gives.

[^how-no-false-echoes]: No English turn that calls up a better-known story the source doesn't mean: Christ leads Adam and Eve out of hell, never "casts them out", which calls up Eden. An echo the source means stays.

[^how-read-aloud]: Every sentence or line was read aloud and its slack cut with no fact dropped; prose stays in paragraphs, and verse keeps its lines and stanzas, and its beat and rhyme wherever they survive.

[^how-scenes-read-for-movement]: Each whole scene was read for what each speaker wants and where it turns, and the whole book in order, so the dialogue and paragraph breaks carry it; nothing the source doesn't give was added.

[^how-sources-named]: Every reading says whose it is: the scholars who edited the text by name, the printing by its year, the handwritten copy by its own name, and this translation's own work as "this translation", never "we".

[^how-honest-where-unsure]: Where the dictionaries leave a passage open, it's translated as the scholars who edited the text read it, and the doubt is given in an endnote, never smoothed over.

[^how-no-borrowed-wording]: Nothing was translated from Malory's English or any modern translation; they were used only to find where a passage sits in the legend.

## Nazirites

Also: Nazaraei

People of Israel vowed to God, who let their hair grow and drank no wine.<sup>[\*](GLOSSARY.md#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

## Nebat

Also: Nabath

The father of King Jeroboam in the Bible, known only as his father.<sup>[\*](GLOSSARY.md#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

## Nicolaus

Also: Nicolaum

A man Gildas takes for the founder of a heresy, the Nicolaitans the Book of Revelation condemns.<sup>[\*](GLOSSARY.md#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

Wikipedia: [Nicolaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaism)

## Nineveh

Also: Ninivitis

The capital of Assyria, spared by God when its people repented at Jonah's warning.<sup>[\*](GLOSSARY.md#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

Wikipedia: [Nineveh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nineveh)

## Novatus

Also: Novatus

Gildas's name for Novatian, a Roman priest who split the church at Rome in the third century.<sup>[\*](GLOSSARY.md#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

Wikipedia: [Novatian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novatian)

## Oded

Also: Obed

The father of the prophet Azariah in the Bible, printed as Obed by Mommsen.<sup>[\*](GLOSSARY.md#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

Wikipedia: [Oded, father of Azariah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oded,_father_of_Azariah)

## Parthians

Also: Parthorum

The people of Parthia, in today's northeastern Iran, Rome's great rival in the east.<sup>[\*](GLOSSARY.md#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

Wikipedia: [Parthia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthia)

## Pharisees

Also: Pharisaei

A party of Jewish teachers in the Gospels.<sup>[\*](GLOSSARY.md#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

Wikipedia: [Pharisees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharisees)

## Philistines

Also: Philistinorum

A people on the coast of Canaan in the Bible, Israel's enemies.<sup>[\*](GLOSSARY.md#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

Wikipedia: [Philistines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philistines)

## Philo

Also: Philonis

Philo of Alexandria, a Jewish writer of the first century CE.<sup>[\*](GLOSSARY.md#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

Wikipedia: [Philo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philo)

## Phinehas

Also: Finees

The name of two priests Gildas names: Phinehas, Aaron's grandson, who punished fornication in Israel, and Phinehas son of Eli, who died on the same day as his brother Hophni.<sup>[\*](GLOSSARY.md#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

Wikipedia: [Phinehas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phinehas)

## Picts

Also: Picti, Pictorum, Pictorumque

A people of the far north of Britain who raid the Britons from the sea.<sup>[\*](GLOSSARY.md#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

Wikipedia: [Picts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picts)

## Polycarp

Also: Polycarpus

A bishop of Smyrna, burned for his faith in the second century.<sup>[\*](GLOSSARY.md#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

Wikipedia: [Polycarp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polycarp)

## Porphyry of Tyre

Also: Porphyrius

A Greek philosopher of the third century CE who wrote against the Christians.<sup>[\*](GLOSSARY.md#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

Wikipedia: [Porphyry of Tyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porphyry_of_Tyre)

The people of Rome and its empire, who rule Britain in Gildas's history.<sup>[\*](GLOSSARY.md#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

The city in Italy that rules the empire.

Wikipedia: [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rome)

The capital of the northern kingdom of Israel in the Bible, and the land around it.<sup>[\*](GLOSSARY.md#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

A strong man of the Bible, who pulled down a Philistine temple on himself and his enemies.<sup>[\*](GLOSSARY.md#new-in-this-translation)</sup>
