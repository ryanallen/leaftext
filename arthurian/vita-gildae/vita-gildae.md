# The Life of Gildas

*The Life of Gildas*, by Caradoc of Llancarfan, translated by [Ryan Allen](vita-gildae-how-it-was-made.md#ryan-allen)

[All the Arthurian books](../index.md) · **Read in:** [UK English](uk/vita-gildae-uk.md)

## About this translation

**Written:** 1100–1165 CE

Caradoc of Llancarfan wrote *The Life of Gildas* in Latin sometime in 1100–1165 CE, six hundred years after Gildas wrote *On the Ruin of Britain*. Caradoc's Gildas lives in Arthur's time. Arthur hunts down Gildas's brother Hueil, a raider who obeys no king, and kills him on the Isle of Man; then he does penance, and Gildas forgives him. Later Arthur besieges Glastonbury to win back Guinevere from King Melwas, who has carried her off, until the abbot and Gildas persuade Melwas to give her back. It's the oldest telling of the queen carried off, which the later romances tell again. Caradoc calls Arthur king of all Britain, but a tyrant and a rebel too.

**The text.** The *Life* was written in the twelfth century CE, before 1166 CE, the year of its oldest dated copy, at Durham. This translation follows the Latin as Theodor Mommsen printed it in 1898 from Cambridge, Corpus Christi College, MS 139, of the twelfth century, with the readings of British Museum Burney 310, copied from the Durham manuscript in 1381 CE. An earlier life of Gildas, by a monk of Rhuys in Brittany, names no Arthur, and isn't translated here.

## Part 1: King Nau's son Gildas studies in Gaul, teaches in Britain, and stops mid-sermon

### Gildas, one of King Nau's twenty-four sons, is given to his studies

<a id="page-107"></a>[\[Mommsen 107\]](vita-gildae-latin.md#page-107) Nau was king of Scotland, the noblest of the northern rulers.[^107-1] He had twenty-four sons, all of them warlike and victorious. One was named Gildas, and his parents gave him over to the study of letters.[^107-2] The boy had a good nature and loved to learn, and his mind flourished. Whatever he heard from his teacher he remembered with great care, and he never forgot it. He worked hard among his fellows at the seven arts until he reached his youth.[^107-3] Once he was a young man, he soon left the country.

### Gildas studies seven years in Gaul and comes home to teach in Britain

He crossed the sea to Gaul and stayed in its cities, studying very well, for seven years. At the end of the seventh year he came back to Greater Britain with a great load of books of every kind.[^107-4] The famous newcomer's name spread, and students flocked to him in crowds from all sides. They learned the seven arts from him in fine detail, and from pupils they became masters, with a master's honor.

### Gildas lives as a hermit, fasting, praying and sleeping on bare rock

All the Britons praised and honored the wise teacher's devotion so much that his equal was never found, and couldn't be, for his great merits. He fasted like Anthony the hermit.[^107-5] He prayed in a hair shirt. Whatever he was given he spent on the poor at once. He kept from sweet milk and from honey. He hated meat, and liked cress from the brook better. He ate barley bread mixed with ashes and drank spring water every day. He didn't go into the baths, which his people loved most. His face looked thin, so he seemed like someone with a bad fever.

At midnight he used to go into the river and stand there without moving until he'd said the Lord's Prayer three times. After that he went back to his chapel. There he knelt and prayed to God's majesty until full daylight. He slept little, lying on a rock dressed in a single garment. He ate without ever eating his fill, satisfied only by earning heaven's reward. That was what he longed for.

### Gildas preaches across Britain, and in Pebidiog his voice is held back

He taught people to scorn what passes in a moment. He was a famous preacher across Britain's three kingdoms. Kings feared him, as they should, and obeyed him when they listened to his welcome preaching. Every Sunday he preached at a church by the sea, which stands in the district of Pebidiog, in the time of King Trifinus, and a countless crowd heard him.[^107-6]

Once, as he began to preach, his voice was held back in him, and the people were amazed at it. When Saint Gildas found this, he told everyone there to go out, to learn if one of them was keeping him from preaching God's word. Even after they'd left, he couldn't preach. Then he asked whether anyone, man or woman, was hiding in the church.

<a id="page-108"></a>[\[Mommsen 108\]](vita-gildae-latin.md#page-108) Saint Non answered him. She was pregnant, and would be the mother of the holy child David. "It's me," she said. "I'm staying here between the wall and the door, since I didn't want to go in among the crowd." When he heard this, he told her to go out. Once she'd gone, he called the people back, and they came to hear the gospel preached.

When the sermon was over, he asked God's angel about what had happened: why he'd begun to speak and couldn't finish. The messenger revealed it to him. "Saint Non, a holy woman, is in the church," he said. "She's pregnant now, and will bear a boy of great grace. Because of him you couldn't preach, since God's power held back your words. The child who's coming will be more blessed than you. He'll have no equal in your part of the world."

Then Gildas said: "I'll leave this region to him. He'll grow quickly and flourish from one age to the next. God's angel, his messenger, has told me that this is truly what's meant to be."[^108-1]

## Part 2: Arthur hunts Gildas's brother Hueil, then meets Gildas, who takes a bell to Rome

### Gildas goes to Ireland, and Arthur pursues Gildas's rebellious brother Hueil

So it happened that the holy preacher Gildas crossed to Ireland, where he turned countless people to the Catholic faith.[^108-2] He lived in the time of Arthur, king of all Greater Britain. Gildas loved him, as was right, and always wanted to obey him. But his twenty-three brothers stood against Arthur as a rebel. They wouldn't put up with a lord. They kept routing Arthur, driving him out of forest and field.

Hueil, the eldest, was a constant fighter and a famous soldier. He obeyed no one, not even Arthur. He harassed him, and stirred up the fiercest fury between the two of them. He came very often from Scotland, set fires, and carried off plunder in triumph and to great praise.

The ruler of all Britain heard that the bold young man had done these things and was doing more like them. So he hunted him down. Hueil had won so many fights and was the best of them. The people of the land said and hoped he'd be their king one day. In that pursuit, and a battle on the Isle of Man, Arthur killed the young raider. After the killing he came home the victor, very glad to have beaten his strongest enemy.

Gildas, the Britons' historian, was still in Ireland then, running a school and preaching in the city of Armagh.[^108-3] He heard that King Arthur had killed his brother. The news hurt him, and he cried and groaned, mourning him as only family can. He prayed for Hueil's spirit every day. He asked mercy for Arthur too, the man who'd hunted his kin down and taken his life. So he kept the command given to the apostles: "Pray for those who persecute you, and do good to those who hate you."[^108-4]

### Gildas brings a bell to Britain and meets Arthur before the bishops

Meanwhile the holy Gildas, that honored historian, came to Britain carrying a very beautiful bell with a very sweet sound. He'd vowed to present it to the pope of the Roman church. He spent the night in the valley of Llancarfan, received with honor by Cadoc, its venerable abbot.[^108-5] Gildas showed him the fine bell. Cadoc took it and looked it over, then wanted to buy it for a great price, but its owner wouldn't sell.

King Arthur and the leading men of all Britain learned that Gildas the Wise had come. Bishops and abbots met with countless clergy and people. They came to make peace between Arthur and Gildas over the killing. Gildas did as he'd done at first, when he got the news of his brother's death. He forgave his enemy, kissed the man who asked his pardon, and blessed him with all goodwill. After that, King Arthur, sorry and in tears, took a penance from the bishops there. He made amends as far as he could, to the end of his life.

### Gildas takes the bell to Rome and offers it to the pope

From there the good Gildas, a man of peace and of the Catholic faith, went to Rome. He gave the bell to the pope of the Roman church. But when the pontiff shook it, it made no sound. Seeing this, he said: "Man loved by God and by people, tell me what happened to this gift on your way."

Gildas told him that the holy Cadoc, abbot of the church of Llancarfan, had wanted to buy it. But Gildas had vowed to offer it to Saint Peter the apostle, and wouldn't sell it. When he heard this, the pope said: "I know Cadoc, that venerable man.

<a id="page-109"></a>[\[Mommsen 109\]](vita-gildae-latin.md#page-109) "He's come to this city seven times, and to Jerusalem three times, through terrible dangers and constant labor. Now that you're back, I allow you to give it to him, since he wants it. This miracle shows that it's meant for him."

So Gildas took the blessed bell back and went home. He carried it back and gave it to Saint Cadoc for nothing. As soon as the abbot held it in his hands and struck it, it rang out, and everyone wondered at it. From then on it served as a refuge for all who carried it through the whole of Wales. Whoever swore a false oath on it either lost the use of their tongue or at once confessed the wrong they'd done.

## Part 3: Gildas teaches at Llancarfan, then lives alone on an island beside Cadoc's

### Gildas teaches a year at Cadoc's school and copies the four Gospels

Cadoc, abbot of the church of Llancarfan, asked the teacher Gildas to run the school for a year.[^109-1] At his request Gildas ran it to great profit, taking no pay from the students except the prayers of the clergy and the scholars. There he wrote out the four Gospels himself. The book is still in Saint Cadoc's church, covered all over with gold and silver, in honor of God, of its holy scribe and of the Gospels. The Welsh of the country swear their oaths on that precious volume. They don't dare open it to look inside. Nor will they seal peace and friendship between enemies unless it's first laid out in front of them.

### Gildas and Cadoc leave Llancarfan for two islands, Steep Holm and Flat Holm

When the year was over and the students had left the school, the holy abbot Cadoc and Gildas, the best of teachers, agreed together to go to two islands, Steep Holm and Flat Holm. Cadoc went to the one nearer Wales, and Gildas to the one lying toward England.[^109-2] They didn't want crowds of people keeping them from the church's services. So they knew no better plan than to leave the valley of Llancarfan for a hidden life on the islands.

Gildas built a chapel in honor of the holy and undivided Trinity, and his cell beside it. But he didn't keep his bed in that room. It lay under a high cliff, where he stayed awake until midnight, praying to almighty God on the rock. Then he went to the church, frozen through, but the cold was sweet and bearable to him for God's sake. He drew little fish from his net and took eggs from birds' nests, and lived on them, which was food enough for him. The two of them visited each other. They went on that way for seven years.

## Part 4: Pirates drive Gildas to Glastonbury, where Arthur besieges Melwas for Guinevere

### Pirates from Orkney drive Gildas to Glastonbury, which Arthur besieges for Guinevere

The highest Creator saw that Gildas, his beloved servant, had no lasting water except the raindrops that fell on the rocks and were caught as they dropped.[^109-3] So he made a stream run from the high cliff. It flowed, it flows, and it'll go on without ever failing. While Saint Gildas went on so, intent on fasting and prayer, pirates came from the Orkney Islands. They harassed him, taking the servants who looked after him and carrying them off into exile, with their spoils and all the goods of his house.

He was so badly troubled that he couldn't live there any longer. He left the island, got into a small boat and came to Glastonbury in great grief, while Melwas ruled the Summer Country. The abbot of Glastonbury welcomed him, as such a man deserved. Gildas taught the brothers and people of all kinds, sowing the seed of heaven's teaching. There he wrote his histories of Britain's kings.[^109-4]

Glastonbury, that is, the City of Glass, which took its name from glass, was a city first named in the British tongue. Arthur the tyrant besieged it with a countless host, because of Guinevere, his wife.[^109-5] The wicked man we've spoken of had violated her and carried her off. He'd brought her there for refuge in a sanctuary no one had ever broken into, guarded by its banks of reeds, its river and its marsh. The rebel king had searched for the queen for a whole year.[^109-6] At last he heard she was there. At once he called up the armies of all Cornwall and Devon, and the enemies made ready for war.[^109-7]

### The abbot and Gildas go between the armies to speak to Melwas

When the abbot of Glastonbury saw this, he went out between the battle lines with his clergy and Gildas the Wise. He urged his lord, Melwas, to make peace and give back the woman he'd taken. So she was handed back, as she had to be, with goodwill on both sides. After this the two kings gave the abbey much land. They came to visit Saint Mary's church and to pray there.

<a id="page-110"></a>[\[Mommsen 110\]](vita-gildae-latin.md#page-110) The abbot confirmed the brotherhood they loved, for the truce they'd made, for the gifts they'd given, and for more they'd give later. Then the kings went home reconciled. They promised to obey the most reverend head of Glastonbury, and never to violate the holy place, or even the lands that belonged to it.

## Part 5: Gildas falls ill at Glastonbury, and Caradoc explains the town's two names

### Gildas lives as a hermit again beside a river near Glastonbury

With leave from the abbot of Glastonbury, his clergy and his people, the devout Gildas wanted to live a hermit's life again, on the bank of a river near the town.[^110-1] He did as he wished. There he built a church in the name of the holy and undivided Trinity. In it he fasted and prayed without rest, dressed in a hair shirt, and gave everyone a blameless example of living well in faith. Saintly men came from far parts of Britain to visit him, as he deserved. They went home with his warnings in mind, and kept the encouragement and advice they'd heard with joy.

### Gildas falls ill and calls for the abbot of Glastonbury

At last he fell ill. As the sickness grew worse, he sent for the abbot of Glastonbury. With great devotion he begged him to have his body carried to the abbey there, which he loved most, when his life was over. His friend promised to do what the worthy saint wanted, and was sorry at the news, and cried hard. Then the holy Gildas died after his long illness. Many saw an angelic glow around his fragrant body, and angels going with his soul.

When the prayers for him had been said in tears, the brothers carried his remains, which weighed very little, to the abbey. With great mourning and the highest honor he was buried in the middle of the floor of Saint Mary's church. His soul was at peace in heaven then, is now, and always will be. Amen.

### Caradoc explains Glastonbury's British and English names, and signs his work

Long ago Glastonbury was called *Ynisgutrin*, and the Britons of the land still call it that. In the British tongue *ynis* is the Latin *insula*, an island, and *gutrin* means of glass. But after the English came and drove out the Britons, that is, the Welsh, it was renamed *Glastigberi*, after its first name: *glas* in English is the Latin *vitrum*, and *beria* is a city. So it's *Glastiberia*, the City of Glass.[^110-2]

These are the writings of Caradoc of Llancarfan. Whoever reads them may correct them, and the writer is content.
