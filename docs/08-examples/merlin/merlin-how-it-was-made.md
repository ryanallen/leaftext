# How it was made

Who made this book, and what each phrase of the how line in its note means: each section says what the phrase means, then opens its file where it has one.

<a id="designed-the-system"></a>

## Who designed the system that made it

The tools that made this book, its checks and the rules they hold were designed as one system, and every line in it came out of that system. When a passage reads wrong, the fix goes into the system and the book is made again, never written in by hand, so it reaches every book on the shelf.

<a id="all-checks-passed"></a>

## All checks passed

Every range was run through the book's own check tool and the shelf's checks, and nothing was ticked done until they passed: a missing page, a broken link, an old word, a word repeated too soon, a wrong mark and a change nobody logged are all refused.

<a id="readable-first"></a>

## Readable first

Each passage is told the way a good English writer would tell it today, then checked against the source so every event, person and act is there and nothing is invented.

<a id="plain-speech"></a>

## Plain speech

No Bible cadence and no stiff old-translation voice: no chains of "and… and… and", no solemn word order, no word nobody says now.

<a id="shaped-for-english"></a>

## Shaped for English

Each sentence is written as English, not in the source's grammar: a verb rather than a noun made of one, never a clause hanging off a clause, and what someone must do before where or why, with nothing lost. Every sentence that says a common verb twice was read and either rewritten or kept with the reason.

<a id="every-link-said"></a>

## Every link said

Where the source joins a cause and its effect with a bare "and", the English says how they connect, with "to", "by", "so", "until" or "because".

<a id="nothing-said-twice"></a>

## Nothing said twice

What the source says twice, a doubled verb or a speech the narration has just told, is said once, unless a speaker repeats it on purpose, and the book's account of its text is given once, its edition and scans cited in full only under Sources.

<a id="the-right-word-each-time"></a>

## The right word each time

A source word with several senses takes the English its moment needs, never one word everywhere and never the vaguest.

<a id="no-word-worn-out"></a>

## No word worn out

No word comes back within forty words of itself, names and the commonest grammar words aside; the second use takes another word or goes.

<a id="modern-idiom"></a>

## Modern idiom

Old English turns give way to the ones people use now, in how people talk to and about each other as much as in rare words: "pregnant", "said goodbye", "my mom". Every word judged old is kept in the shelf's list, and the check refuses each one.

<a id="us-english"></a>

## US English

The reviewed US master uses Merriam-Webster spelling and usage, the article a US reader says, as "an herb", *The Chicago Manual of Style*, and lengths and distances in feet and miles. A later UK edition is made from that master.

<a id="english-word-order"></a>

## English word order

The shelf's list of source word orders that must never show through in the English, as "taken from us everyone" for "taken everyone from us". The check refuses each shape on every page of every book, and a shape found later is added and swept over every page already written.

[Open word-order.tsv](word-order.tsv)

<a id="natural-tenses"></a>

## Natural tenses

The story is told in the simple past wherever the order of events is plain, as English tells it, not in the source's stacked past tenses.

<a id="contractions"></a>

## Contractions

The shelf's list of every long form English shortens when it's said aloud, and when: "who is" to "who's", "do not" to "don't", or "never" with the reason. The check refuses a listed form left long in the text, the headings and the notes.

[Open contractions.tsv](contractions.tsv)

<a id="neutral-tone"></a>

## Neutral tone

No archaism and no color the source doesn't give, but a hard word stays hard.

<a id="gender-neutral"></a>

## Gender neutral

Where the source means anyone, the English says "they", "people" or "anyone", never "he" and never a pair of pronouns; a figure the source names keeps the pronoun the source gives.

<a id="no-false-echoes"></a>

## No false echoes

No English turn that calls up a better-known story the source doesn't mean: Christ leads Adam and Eve out of hell, never "casts them out", which calls up Eden. An echo the source means stays.

<a id="read-aloud"></a>

## Prose and verse read aloud; source lines and stanzas kept

Every sentence or line was read aloud and its slack cut with no fact dropped; prose stays in paragraphs, and verse keeps its lines and stanzas, and its beat and rhyme wherever they survive.

<a id="scenes-read-for-movement"></a>

## Scenes read for movement

Each whole scene was read for what each speaker wants and where it turns, and the whole book in order, so the dialogue and paragraph breaks carry it; nothing the source doesn't give was added.

<a id="checked-against-the-scan"></a>

## Checked against the scan

The source text this translation was made from, established page by page against scans of the printed edition: misread letters restored, damaged words settled and the printed corrections applied, each with a note. Where the machine-read text and the page disagree, the scan wins.

[Open made-with.md](made-with.md)

<a id="sources-named"></a>

## Sources named

Every reading says whose it is: the scholars who edited the text by name, the printing by its year, the handwritten copy by its own name, and this translation's own work as "this translation", never "we".

<a id="honest-where-unsure"></a>

## Honest where unsure

Where the dictionaries leave a passage open, it's translated as the scholars who edited the text read it, and the doubt is given in an endnote, never smoothed over.

<a id="no-borrowed-wording"></a>

## No borrowed wording

Nothing was translated from Malory's English or any modern translation; they were used only to find where a passage sits in the legend.

<a id="source-over-malory"></a>

## Source over Malory

Where Malory tells a passage differently, the source is the story and his version goes in a note; where sources are joined into one book, the one written first leads.

<a id="translated-by-manifest"></a>

## Translated by manifest

The manifest's phrases: every recurring phrase of the source with the one English chosen for it, any other English allowed and when, the page it was first decided on, and why. Phrases are translated first, longest first, so the same words read the same way everywhere.

[Open merlin-manifest-phrases.tsv](merlin-manifest-phrases.tsv)

<a id="every-choice-logged"></a>

## Every choice logged

The manifest's words: every recurring source word with its English, the other English each moment allows, the page it was first decided on, the reason, the dictionary it rests on, and every page logged as an exception.

[Open merlin-manifest-words.tsv](merlin-manifest-words.tsv)

<a id="changes-carried-everywhere"></a>

## Changes carried everywhere

The change log: every change made to a page after it was translated, with its time, its kind, its pages, the English before and after, and why. A changed choice is made in the manifest first and carried to every page it reaches, and the check refuses a page that changed with no change logged.

[Open merlin-changes.tsv](merlin-changes.tsv)

<a id="story-headings"></a>

## Story headings

Every heading says what happens in its section, in a short plain sentence, so the headings alone tell the story, without giving away how a fight, a trial, a prophecy or a secret turns out.

<a id="matching-outlines"></a>

## Matching outlines

The source text carries the same headings as the translation, in English, at the same places, so the two outlines match one for one and a heading in either opens the same passage.

[Open made-with.md](made-with.md)

<a id="laid-out-for-screens"></a>

## Laid out for screens

Laid out as a modern book read on a screen: a new speaker starts a new paragraph, no paragraph runs past 130 words, and the punctuation is a US book's.

<a id="pages-linked-to-scans"></a>

## Pages linked to scans

Every page marker in the translation links to the same page of the source text, and each page there opens that page of the scan, kept two ways: a link to where it was found online, and a copy saved beside the book in case that site goes down. Any paragraph can be checked against the page it came from.

[Open merlin-scans.md](merlin-scans.md)

<a id="endnotes"></a>

## Endnotes

The endnotes at the end of the book: what a reader would ask about a custom, a reference or a word, where Malory keeps or changes a passage, and where the source text is uncertain.

[Open merlin.md](merlin.md#endnotes)

<a id="malory-cross-referenced"></a>

## Malory cross-referenced

Where Malory's *Le Morte d'Arthur* tells the same passage, an endnote names his book and chapters; where it doesn't, nothing is said.

<a id="additions-marked"></a>

## Additions marked

A note or glossary entry this translation adds, rather than one the printed edition gives, ends on an asterisk that links to the line saying so, so a reader always knows whose explanation it is.

[Open merlin.md](merlin.md#new-in-this-translation)

<a id="sourced-glossary"></a>

## Sourced glossary

The glossary of the people, places and words the book uses: each entry comes from the printed edition's own index or word list, or is marked as added, with the page it was found on, and its terms are underlined in the text.

[Open GLOSSARY.md](GLOSSARY.md)

<a id="modern-names"></a>

## Modern names

Each person and place goes by the name a modern English reader knows, the one a standard reference such as Wikipedia files them under, in the story and the glossary alike, with the source's own spellings listed under the glossary heading.

[Open GLOSSARY.md](GLOSSARY.md)

<a id="places-located-from-sources"></a>

## Places located from sources

Each place in the glossary says whether it's real, legendary or uncertain, and where it is, from a geographic source, or why no identification is secure.

[Open GLOSSARY.md](GLOSSARY.md)

<a id="no-guessed-identities"></a>

## No guessed identities

When a figure might be a better-known person from another book, the glossary says so only where the sources do; it never guesses.

<a id="no-common-words-glossed"></a>

## No common words glossed

No ordinary English word is a glossary entry, since an entry is underlined on every page of every book that shares the glossary.
