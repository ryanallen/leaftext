# The Welsh Annals

*Annales Cambriae*, translated by [Ryan Allen](annales-cambriae-how-it-was-made.md#ryan-allen)

**Read in:** [UK English](uk/annales-cambriae-uk.md)

## About this translation

The *Welsh Annals* are a list of years from 444 to 977 CE, each with what a Welsh scribe thought worth keeping, and they're the first record to give a year to Arthur's battles: Badon in 516 and Camlann in 537. Every year in this translation is CE. They were put together in the second half of the tenth century and survive in one copy, London, British Library, Harley MS 3859, among the appendices of the *History of the Britons*. This translation follows Egerton Phillimore's letter-for-letter printing of that copy in 1888.

**The years.** Harley MS 3859 gives no dates. It writes *an'*, short for *annus*, "year," once for every year, numbers every tenth, and writes an entry beside some of them. Each entry opens on the year Phillimore printed beside it, which he took from earlier printings of the annals, and the years with nothing written beside them are left out. His count isn't perfectly even: he sets 444 beside the first year, which his own later dates make 445, and his notes point out where the copyist wrote a year too many or too few.

**Names.** People and places go by the names an English reader knows, as Columba and Mordred, and the glossary lists every spelling Harley MS 3859 gives them.

**The History of the Britons.** In Harley MS 3859 the annals come after the [*History of the Britons*](../historia-brittonum/historia-brittonum.md), the Latin history that lists Arthur's twelve battles, also translated on this shelf.

[^153-1]: In the whole story: [Pope Leo changes the date of Easter, and Brigid is born](../arthurian-shelf/story-map.md#pope-leo-changes-easter).<sup>[\*](#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

[^153-2]: The Latin says Easter was moved *cum papa leone*, "with Pope Leo." Phillimore takes *cum* for a slip in putting the Old Welsh *cant*, "by" or "with," into Latin, and the English follows him: "Pope Leo, bishop of Rome, makes the change."

[^153-3]: The Latin says Patrick *ad dominum migratur*, travels to the Lord, the annals' way of telling a saint's death. The *History of the Britons* tells how Patrick was sent to preach to the Irish.<sup>[\*](#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

[^153-4]: Benignus followed Patrick as head of the church at Armagh, in Ireland. The Latin names his death *quies*, a rest, as the annals often name a churchman's.<sup>[\*](#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

[^153-5]: The Latin says Ebur *pausat in Christo*, literally pauses in Christ, the annals' way of telling a churchman's or a king's death. Three hundred fifty years is the age Harley MS 3859 gives, and the annals don't explain it.<sup>[\*](#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

[^154-1]: In the whole story: [Arthur leads the Britons' kings in twelve battles against the Saxons](../arthurian-shelf/story-map.md#arthurs-twelve-battles).<sup>[\*](#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

[^154-2]: The annals mean an actual cross, carried into battle as a standard, not the saying "carry your cross." Some readers think it was painted on Arthur's shield, since Welsh *ysgwyd*, "shield," is close to *ysgwydd*, "shoulder." The *History of the Britons* makes Badon the last of Arthur's twelve battles and has him carry an image of Mary on his shoulders at another, Fort Guinnion, which may be the same shield. *Domini nostri*, "our Lord," is the Church's name for Christ, which English still uses.<sup>[\*](#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

[^154-3]: Harley MS 3859 writes *Qui es*, which Phillimore reads as *quies*, "rest," the annals' word for a churchman's death, and the English follows him: "Saint Brigid dies."

[^154-4]: In the whole story: [Arthur fights Mordred's host and is carried to Avalon](../arthurian-shelf/story-map.md#the-last-battle). Malory tells this in [Book XXI, chapter 4](../malory-sources/malory-map.tsv). There, Arthur and Mordred lead two hosts against each other, and each gives the other his death wound; the annals say only that both fell, not which side each was on or who struck the blow. *Gueith Camlann* is Old Welsh for "the battle of Camlann."<sup>[\*](#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

[^154-5]: Phillimore notes that the end of this entry, from *inbrittannia*, "in Britain," on, is written in Harley MS 3859 over letters scraped out, some of which still show.

[^154-6]: In the whole story: [A great plague comes, and the northern Britons fight at Arfderydd](../arthurian-shelf/story-map.md#a-great-plague-and-the-battle-of-arfderydd).<sup>[\*](#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

[^154-7]: The Latin is *dormitatio*, a falling asleep, the annals' word for a saint's death, as the Church spoke of the dead sleeping until the Resurrection.<sup>[\*](#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

[^155-1]: Maelgwn is the great king of Gwynedd the *History of the Britons* names in the time of the English kings' wars. Welsh tradition remembers this plague as the Yellow Plague. Phillimore reads *guenedotae* for the *genedotae*, "of Gwynedd," of Harley MS 3859.

[^155-2]: Columba left Ireland and founded the monastery on Iona, an island off the west coast of what's now Scotland.<sup>[\*](#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

[^155-3]: Gildas wrote *On the Ruin of Britain*, a sermon against the Britons' kings, which names the siege of Mount Badon but not Arthur.<sup>[\*](#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

[^155-4]: Later Welsh poems set Myrddin, the Welsh Merlin, at this battle, where he loses his mind and flees into the forest; Geoffrey of Monmouth's *Life of Merlin* tells of a battle that drives Merlin mad, without naming it. Phillimore notes an *an'* scraped out just before *armterid*, "Arfderydd," on the same line.<sup>[\*](#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

[^155-5]: Welsh genealogies make Gwrgi and Peredur brothers, sons of Eliffer, kings in the north of Britain. A Welsh romance gives the name Peredur to its hero, who stands for Percival; the annals' Peredur is a king who died in 580 CE.<sup>[\*](#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

[^156-1]: Harley MS 3859 writes *dispositio*, which Phillimore reads as *depositio*, a saint's burial, and the English follows him: "Deiniol of Bangor is buried."

[^156-2]: The annals don't say which King Constantine turned to God. Gildas rebukes a Constantine of Dumnonia, in the southwest of Britain, and Geoffrey of Monmouth makes a Constantine king after Arthur.<sup>[\*](#new-in-this-translation)</sup>

[^156-3]: The Latin runs the two names together, *Agustinus mellitus*, with no "and." Both were monks Pope Gregory sent to the English: Augustine became the first archbishop of Canterbury, and Mellitus the first bishop of London.<sup>[\*](#new-in-this-translation)</sup>
